Choosing your first wine

Five steps for choosing a first wine by occasion, colour, sweetness, strength and budget, with label checks, serving temperatures, pairing notes and common mistakes.

The difficulty of a wine shelf is not that there are too many wines, but that the label carries too much information. For a first purchase you do not need every term. Filter by occasion, colour, sweetness, strength and budget, then use the label to confirm identity, and most pitfalls disappear.

Decision checklist Run these three checks before you buy or go.

  • Fix the occasion first: Everyday drinking, food pairing, a party and a gift call for different choices.
  • Colour and type: Wine is commonly divided into red wine, white wine, sparkling wine and fortified wine.
  • Sweetness, strength and volume: Sweetness is not a scale of quality.

Fix the occasion first

Everyday drinking, food pairing, a party and a gift call for different choices. For everyday drinking, start with a single bottle in a everyday price band; for food, decide the dish first; for a party, consider whether the bottle will be finished; for a gift, consider packaging and a gift box. Once the occasion is fixed, colour and type become much easier.

For a first purchase, do not buy several different types at once. Buy one bottle, note what you like or dislike, then buy a second for comparison. This builds your own preference faster than buying a whole recommended list.

Colour and type

Wine is commonly divided into red wine, white wine, sparkling wine and fortified wine. Red wine ferments with the skins and usually has tannin and structure; white wine ferments without skins and tastes fresher; sparkling wine has bubbles and suits celebrations; fortified wine has higher alcohol and a denser flavour.

Type Typical flavours Serving temperature Suitable occasion
Dry red Black fruit, structure 16–18°C Red meat, stews, family meals
Dry white Citrus, green apple, freshness 8–12°C Seafood, lighter dishes
Sparkling Bubbles, acidity, lightness 6–8°C Celebrations, starters
Fortified Dried fruit, nuts, richness 14–18°C After a meal, small glasses

For a first bottle, choose either dry red or dry white. For dry red, look at Cabernet Sauvignon; for dry white, look at Chardonnay. Both are widely planted and span many styles, which makes comparison easier.

Sweetness, strength and volume

Sweetness is not a scale of quality. Dry, off-dry, semi-sweet and sweet are style choices; if unsure, dry or off-dry is safer for a first purchase, and sweetness should not be confused with quality. Alcohol by volume is usually 11%–15%; higher alcohol brings more heat, lower alcohol feels lighter.

The common volume is 750 mL, with small bottles also available. A small bottle suits one person, while a standard bottle suits a gathering. An opened red usually keeps for a few days, white and sparkling for less; sparkling loses its bubbles quickly and is best finished in one sitting.

Five label checks

  1. Check the type: red, white, sparkling or fortified, to fix the broad category.
  2. Check the variety: Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay and others shape the flavour.
  3. Check the region: country and region names offer style clues, such as France and Bordeaux.
  4. Check the vintage: it is the grape harvest year, not the time in bottle.
  5. Check strength, net volume, producer and importer details.

The vintage only states the harvest year and does not mean older is better. Most everyday wines are best drunk within a few years, and only some structured wines reward long cellaring. For a first purchase, favour fresh bottles from reliable channels.

Budget and buying

When the budget is limited, focus on the right type, good storage and a reliable channel rather than packaging. Reputable supermarkets, brand shops and authorised merchants are safer; protect bottles from light and heat, and lay cork-closed bottles down for long storage.

For a concrete reference, see Penfolds Bin 389: a Cabernet and Shiraz blend with strong structure that suits red meat and serves as a comparison point for "structure". It is not the only answer for beginners, only a coordinate for comparison.

Temperature, glass and pairing

Red wine is served at about 16–18°C, white at about 8–12°C and sparkling at about 6–8°C. Too warm amplifies alcohol, too cold closes the aroma. Without a dedicated glass, a clean glass with a wide bowl and a slightly narrowed rim is enough, as covered in how to choose a glass.

The principle of pairing is weight matching: light dishes with light wines, rich dishes with structured wines. Dry red suits red meat and stews, dry white suits seafood and poultry, and sparkling suits starters and fried food. For a first attempt, do not chase complex pairings; keep the intensity of the wine and the dish close.

How to drink the first glass

Look at the colour, smell, then taste. Smell at rest first, then swirl gently; let the wine cover the tongue and note acidity, tannin, body and finish. You can record it with the method in the first sip without rushing into terminology.

Do not fill the glass for the first pour; keep it below the widest part of the bowl so there is room to swirl and gather aroma. Before drinking, check for off-notes such as wet cardboard, vinegar or obvious oxidation; if in doubt, taste a small amount rather than finishing the glass.

Common mistakes

First, treating price as quality. Second, treating older vintages as better. Third, assuming sweet wine is inferior. Fourth, using a red-wine glass for everything. Fifth, leaving an opened bottle at room temperature for a week. Sixth, reading only the front label and ignoring the producer and importer on the back.

Advanced judgment

Figure: A white wine tasting glass; a first wine starts with glass shape, temperature and aroma. Image: Tim Parkinson, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page).

Occasion Safer choice Pitfall to avoid
Drinking alone Dry white or light-bodied red High alcohol, heavy oak, packaging over substance
Family dinner Medium-acidity white or easy red Too sweet, too heavy, flavours fighting the food
As a gift Clear region, vintage and producer Buying a gift box without checking the wine or storage
In a restaurant Try by the glass, then order a bottle Opening a bottle before checking temperature

Must the first bottle be red? No. White wine is usually easier with food and easier to serve at the right temperature; red tannin and body can clash with lighter dishes. A bottle you will actually finish matters more than one that looks impressive.

Is older always better? No. Most entry-level wines are meant to be drunk young; only wines with enough structure, acidity and concentration repay cellaring. Storage temperature, humidity and light matter more than the number on the label.

Are supermarket wines always bad? Not necessarily, but a shorter chain and faster turnover usually mean more controllable condition. Check the producer, vintage, importer and back label, and avoid bottles stored upright in light or heat.
