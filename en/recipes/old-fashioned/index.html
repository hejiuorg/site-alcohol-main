This is one Old Fashioned recipe with a stated source. The Old Fashioned is one of the few classic cocktails built entirely in the glass: sugar, bitters, water and whisky go into the same glass in order and are stirred there, with no shaking and no straining. This recipe records that version's ratio and sequence and does not mix other practices into it.

A recipe with a stated source

The public IBA (International Bartenders Association) recipe uses 45 ml of bourbon or rye whisky, one sugar cube, a couple of dashes of Angostura bitters and a little water, built in a rocks glass and garnished with an orange slice or peel. This recipe follows that version and explains the purpose of each step so that a problem at home can be traced to a specific stage.

The recipe version is recorded separately from the cocktail identity so that it stays traceable. The Old Fashioned has been made differently in different decades and different bars: some use syrup and some a sugar cube; some add soda water and some only a few drops of water; some garnish with orange peel and some with a cherry. These differences are not about who is "authentic"; they are different versions and should carry their own sources.

Ingredients and ratio

  • Bourbon or rye whisky: 45 ml
  • Sugar cube: 1
  • Angostura bitters: 2 dashes
  • Water: about 5 ml
  • Ice: as needed
  • Orange peel or slice: 1 (optional: 1 cocktail cherry)

This version has no fruit juice, no carbonated drink and no syrup. Its whole structure is "spirit plus a little sugar, a little water and bitters". The ratio looks loose, yet each element changes the direction of the drink: sugar sets sweetness and body, water sets the starting point of dilution, and bitters set the breadth of aroma.

Quantities are a starting point, not a law. Glass size, the amount of ice and the strength of the whisky all change the result. Make the first drink to the numbers on this recipe, then adjust one variable at a time; otherwise you cannot tell where a change came from.

What each ingredient does

Whisky is the body: it supplies grain aroma, sweetness and the alcoholic frame. Bourbon is corn-led and usually sweeter and rounder; whisky with a high rye share is spicier and drier. Changing the base spirit clearly changes the drink, but the structure stays the same and it remains in the Old Fashioned family.

The sugar cube supplies sweetness and, once dissolved, a little volume. Too little sugar leaves the drink hard and the bitters dominant; too much covers the detail of the whisky. The sugar must be mostly dissolved before the ice goes in, or the sweetness will concentrate in the last few sips.

Bitters supply aroma and a bitter edge. The quantity is tiny, yet it lifts the sweetness of the whisky and keeps the drink from being flat. Bitter brands differ in recipe and aromatic direction; this recipe uses Angostura only as the reference for this version and does not rank other brands.

The role of water is often underestimated. A few drops open up a high-strength whisky and make the aroma clearer; too much water thins the body. The amount should follow the strength of the whisky, not a fixed number.

Method

First put the sugar cube in a rocks glass, add the bitters and a little water, and crush and stir it gently with a bar spoon or muddler until the sugar has mostly dissolved. Do not skip this step: a whole cube left at the bottom dissolves even more slowly once ice is added, and the last sips turn suddenly sweet.

Then add ice up to just below the rim, pour in the whisky and stir gently for some ten seconds with a bar spoon so that the sugar solution, bitters, water and whisky mix and cool. Keep the motion small; violent stirring makes the ice melt fast. For the general principle, see stirring and cocktail basics.

Ice and dilution

Dilution in an Old Fashioned comes from two places: the small amount of water added on purpose, and the ice melting slowly while you drink. The first is controllable; the second depends on ice shape, room temperature and how fast you drink.

A large cube has little surface area and melts slowly, so the drink stays cold for longer without turning watery; crushed ice chills fast but thins the drink within minutes. With a large cube, cool the whisky slightly first, or the room-temperature liquid will warm the ice and speed up melting.

The real strength of the drink depends on the alcohol by volume of the whisky, the amount of sugar and the amount of ice, not on the millilitres in the recipe alone. For more on ice shapes, see ice and temperature.

Glass and temperature

Traditionally the drink is served in a rocks glass: short and wide, with a rim wide enough for a sugar cube, a bar spoon and a large cube, and a wide mouth that makes nosing easy. The glass has no stem, so holding the body transfers hand heat; to keep it cold longer, hold it near the rim or use a coaster.

Glass capacity affects how the recipe behaves. Too small a glass overflows once ice is added; too large a glass leaves too much headspace and lets the aroma disperse. "Ice up to just below the rim" means adjusting the ice to your own glass, not copying it mechanically.

Garnish

Orange peel supplies citrus oil; expressed over the rim and the surface, it shapes the first impression. An orange slice is more visual. A cocktail cherry adds sweet aroma and recognition but is not essential. The garnish should be finished just before drinking; left too long, citrus peel dries out and loses aroma.

The garnish is there to complement, not to cover. Expressing peel too hard lets the bitter white pith into the drink; too many cherries let sweetness override the whisky. For bright aroma, use peel; for sweet aroma and visual identity, add a cherry; if you use both, use less of each.

Common mistakes

First, adding ice before the sugar has dissolved. Sugar at the bottom turns the last sips suddenly sweet and throws off the balance.

Second, adding too much water. An Old Fashioned is not a diluted whisky; excess water makes the body loose and the aroma closed.

Third, stirring too vigorously. Hard agitation melts the ice quickly and lets dilution run away.

Fourth, filling the glass with crushed ice. A short drink usually suits large ice; crushed ice thins it within minutes.

Fifth, over-garnishing. Orange peel, an orange slice and cherries piled on the rim cover the detail of the whisky.

Version differences and variations

Replacing the cube with syrup dissolves faster and makes sweetness easier to control, but adds extra water; replacing the water with soda water gives a light sparkle and a lighter texture; replacing Angostura with another bitters changes the aromatic direction; replacing whisky with another spirit produces an Old Fashioned-style drink on a different base.

Limits

This recipe records one source version only. It does not rank brands and does not present one sugar, bitters or garnish as the only correct choice. A recipe is a starting point, not a law; any change should be labelled as a difference. Drink in moderation, avoid drinking quickly on an empty stomach, and do not drink while driving, taking medication or pregnant; follow local law.
