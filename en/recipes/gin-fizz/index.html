Gin Fizz recipe (IBA version)

45 ml gin, 30 ml fresh lemon juice and 10 ml sugar syrup are shaken and strained before a splash of soda water goes in; this recipe explains why soda comes last, the dry 45:30:10 frame, the egg-white Silver Fizz and the ways carbonation is lost.

Method
Shaken
Yield
1 drink · 1 serving(s)
Garnish
Lemon slice, optional lemon peel

In a Gin Fizz, soda water is not a footnote at the end of the ingredient list; it is the step that decides the drink's character. The argument behind most bars is not which gin to use but when the soda goes in, how much goes in, and whether to stir after it does. The IBA answer is blunt: shake the gin, lemon and syrup, strain into a tall glass, then add a splash of soda water, with no ice in the glass. The 45:30:10 figures therefore read like a sequence of operations rather than a plain ratio.

Soda water is always the last step

The word "fizz" refers to the bubbles. Adding soda last preserves not only the gas but the way the gas releases aroma: as carbon dioxide rises, it carries lemon and gin notes to the rim, so the drink smells stronger than it tastes. Add the soda before shaking and most of the carbonate is knocked out of solution, and by the time the drink is strained there is little left but a sour with extra water.

The last step also matters for strength. Shaking dilutes the drink, and the soda adds more volume; together they set the final concentration. The IBA says "a splash, to top up", meaning enough to reach near the rim and establish a bubble layer, not enough to fill the glass with soda. The less you add, the more body remains; the more you add, the closer it comes to a soft drink.

In practice, pour the soda slowly at the rim so the level rises on its own, and avoid firing it into the bottom of the glass. No bar-spoon stirring is needed: tilt the glass two or three times and let the layers exchange. If you must stir, use one gentle lift, not a circular motion.

The dry 45:30:10 frame

Gin 45, lemon 30, syrup 10 is more sour than many equal-parts sour recipes. The lemon is close to two thirds of the gin, and the syrup is only a third of the lemon, so the drink enters on acid and reaches the gin's botanicals afterwards, with sweetness appearing only in the finish.

Why design it that way? Because the soda will dilute further and add carbonation. If the shaken base were already sweet, the finished drink would taste like lemonade. Keeping sugar at 10 ml leaves room for the soda: after dilution the acid is still clear, the gin still stands, and sweetness stays in the background.

The numbers also explain why the drink may feel "too sour". If you are used to a syrup-heavy version, the first IBA Gin Fizz tastes sharp; that is not a mistake but a recipe that puts freshness ahead of sweetness. To round it out, cut the lemon before adding sugar, because sugar reads louder in a carbonated drink.

Syrup strength needs the same attention. At the same 10 ml, a 1:1 syrup and a 2:1 rich syrup differ by nearly a factor of two in sweetness. The IBA does not prescribe a sugar-to-water ratio, so taste the finished drink when you change syrup instead of trusting the millilitres.

Shake first, carbonate second: why the order cannot flip

Shaking is violent; carbonate hates violence. That alone settles the order: shake the liquid, then add the bubbles. Without soda in the tin, the ice can do its full chilling work, the syrup can dissolve, and the lemon aroma can be released by the impact. The carbonation step then does one job only: get gas into the drink.

Reverse the order and three things happen. The bubbles are largely lost in the shake; the dilution from shaking stacks on top of the soda volume, leaving the drink thin; and gas inside the tin changes the pressure during shaking, raising the risk of leaks. Together, the drink ends up neither aromatic nor crisp.

Do not shake after adding soda either. Some people give the tin a light shake to "mix" the soda, which spends the very gas they wanted. The correct mixing is natural convection: soda is less dense and sits on top, so tilting the glass a few times exchanges the layers and spreads the bubbles more evenly.

The egg-white version and the Silver Fizz

Adding egg white is usually called a Silver Fizz. It does not change the 45:30:10 structure, but it changes texture and appearance: the foam is thicker, the entry softer, and the bubbles are held by the foam and fade more slowly. It is a variation within the same family and should not be conflated with the IBA version without egg white.

A Silver Fizz needs a dry shake. Shake the gin, lemon, syrup and egg white without ice for a dozen seconds so the protein meets the acid, then add ice and shake to chill. Shake the egg white with ice from the start and the foam is usually thin and loose. Add the soda after the dry shake, and the foam sits above the bubble layer as a two-tier structure.

The usual quantity is half an egg white, about 15 ml. Less than that and the foam cannot hold the rim; more and the texture turns sticky while the gin's botanicals are shut out. Pasteurised egg white foams less readily than fresh and needs a longer dry shake.

Glass, ice and "no ice in the glass"

The IBA specifies a thin tall tumbler; the highball registered on this site belongs to that family, though the exact vessel still follows the official IBA version. The tall, narrow body has a practical purpose: the bubbles travel farther, aroma collects at the rim, and the drink looks like a fizz.

The decisive point is that no ice goes into the serving glass. This is the opposite of a gin-and-tonic style long drink: in the IBA version, ice is used only for shaking and is strained out. Without ice the drink warms steadily and the bubbles decline, so it should be finished quickly; its best moment is the first few minutes after serving.

All the ice therefore belongs to the shake. Too little and the drink is under-chilled, strong and closed; too much with a long shake over-dilutes it, and the later soda makes it taste watery. Hard, large cubes give more control; crushed ice chills faster but melts faster and can thin the body before the soda even goes in.

The glass can be pre-chilled, but do not freeze it until it frosts. Very low glass temperature suppresses the gin's botanicals and makes the bubbles escape faster at the wall. Refrigerate, take it out, dry the outside, and serve.

Six ways the bubbles escape

First, shaking the soda with the drink. This is the most damaging error, because the carbonate is lost to impact. Second, pouring soda hard into the bottom of the glass, which breaks the bubbles during the pour itself. Third, stirring with a bar spoon after topping up, which creates a vortex by hand.

Fourth, a glass that is too wide or too short. A wide rim gives gas a large escape surface; a short body gives the bubbles little distance to travel and the aroma little chance to collect. Fifth, a drink that is too warm. Under-shaking or leaving the strained liquid standing raises the temperature, and carbon dioxide dissolves less readily in warm liquid.

Sixth, soda water that has already gone flat. A bottle opened long ago, or one opened and closed repeatedly, has lost most of its gas; no amount of correct sequencing will build a fizz from it. Use it soon after opening, and do not leave the mouth of the bottle open while you pour.

Other common failures

Beyond the bubbles, several things can go wrong. Too much syrup is the most common: the drink turns sweet and the gin's botanicals and the lemon's freshness are buried, so it tastes like lemon soda. Too little lemon, or bottled juice, is the second: the acid is low and the drink feels loose, while bottled juice lacks fresh peel aroma and tastes flat.

Over-shaking is the third. Too much ice melts, the liquid thins, and after the soda goes in the drink tastes watery. Fourth is the wrong glass: a wide, short vessel lets the gas escape quickly and fails to collect the aroma.

Fifth is a gin that does not match the recipe. The gin share is high, so a gin with clear botanicals makes the drink more layered, while a very soft gin lets acid and bubbles lead. Both work, but they point in different directions, and the style used should be recorded. The actual strength also depends on the gin's alcohol by volume, the volume of syrup and the shaking dilution, not on the recipe millilitres alone.

Variations and the fizz family

Adding egg white is the largest branch, the Silver Fizz; it changes foam and texture, not the sweet-sour frame. Another branch puts ice in the serving glass for a colder, slower-diluting drink, but that clearly differs from the IBA "no ice" version and should be marked as a version difference.

The sweetener can move too. Granulated sugar in place of syrup dissolves more slowly and distributes unevenly, so give it more time before shaking; honey syrup brings floral notes and a longer finish. Replacing soda water with another sparkling water changes bubble size and mineral character, and the drink's style shifts with it.

The fizz family has other members as well: changing the base spirit, adding juice or cutting the sugar all produce different recipes. Each has its own ratio and order and cannot simply borrow the Gin Fizz's 45:30:10. When recording a variation, say which ingredient changed and how the ratio moved, or the same name will describe very different drinks.
