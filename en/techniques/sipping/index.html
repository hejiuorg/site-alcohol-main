Sipping

A tasting technique in which a small amount of liquid covers the mouth for a few seconds to assess sweetness, acidity, body, tannin, alcohol and finish; small sips, short contact and fixed conditions make comparison possible.

Sipping is the central act of tasting: take a small amount of liquid, spread it through the mouth and spend a few seconds judging sweetness, acidity, body, tannin, alcohol and finish. It differs from an everyday gulp because the aim is not thirst but comparable sensory information.

Purpose

The first purpose is assessing structure. Are sweet and acid in balance, is the body light or full, is the tannin coarse, does alcohol stand out? The second is confirming aroma. Many notes only become complete in the mouth, especially those from fermentation and cask ageing. The third is judging the finish: how long flavour persists in the mouth and nose after swallowing or spitting is an important dimension of quality.

Sipping cannot replace nosing, and the first sip cannot settle everything. Liquid warms in the mouth, mixes with saliva and is moved by air, all of which change the impression. Fixed sip size, contact time and recording method are needed before wines can be compared.

Equipment and preparation

A clean glass, a stable room temperature, water and plain crackers are the basic kit. Water rinses the mouth between wines; plain crackers absorb residual oil and flavour, but strongly sweet or salty foods should be avoided because they distort the next wine.

When tasting several wines, keep a sheet or table for sweetness, acidity, body, tannin, alcohol, flavour and finish. The sheet reduces memory bias; from memory alone, the previous wine easily contaminates the next.

Temperature matters greatly. White and sparkling wines are usually served cooler, reds a little warmer, and baijiu and most spirits are often tasted at room temperature. Exact figures vary by drink and preference; what counts is keeping temperature fixed within one comparison and recording it.

Procedure

First, take a small sip of about five to ten millilitres. Too much makes it hard to move the liquid and triggers an instinct to swallow. Second, let the liquid touch the tip of the tongue first, then spread it slowly to the sides and back so different areas register it.

Third, hold it for about two to three seconds rather than swallowing at once. You can open the lips slightly and draw in a little air to mix the liquid with air; aroma then travels from the back of the mouth into the nose. Keep the action gentle to avoid choking. Fourth, feel the weight and texture in the mouth: thin or thick, smooth or rough.

Fifth, swallow or spit into a spittoon. Professional tasting usually spits to stay clear-headed and limit alcohol intake. Sixth, after swallowing or spitting, time the finish and record how long it lasts and what it contains. A finish of a few seconds, or one that is only alcohol heat, is still information worth recording.

Reading the parameters

Sweetness is one of the first sensations. A dry wine is not completely without sweetness; alcohol and glycerol add roundness, while sweetness in a sweet wine may be balanced by high acidity so that it does not feel cloying.

Acidity provides freshness and structure. Too little makes a drink feel loose and flat; too much triggers saliva at the sides of the mouth and can suppress fruit. Body is the weight of the liquid in the mouth, shaped by alcohol, sugar, extract and texture; it cannot be read directly from colour depth.

Tannin comes mainly from grape skins, seeds, stems and oak, and shows as astringency, dryness and texture. The amount and the quality of tannin differ: plenty of fine tannin and plenty of coarse tannin feel completely different. Alcohol often appears as warmth or heat; high alcohol amplifies sweetness and aroma but can mask detail.

Finish is what continues after swallowing or spitting. Length, complexity and cleanliness can all be recorded. Distinguish a long finish from a flavour that simply will not leave: the latter may be alcohol heat, lingering bitterness or a fault.

Failure cases

First, taking too large a sip. A mouthful forces a swallow, prevents the liquid from staying in the mouth and lets alcohol hit all at once. Second, serving too cold. Over-chilling suppresses aroma and sweetness and makes acidity feel sharp.

Third, sipping alongside spicy, sweet or heavily salty food. Food covers the palate and changes saliva, so acidity and tannin are misread. Fourth, tasting continuously without breaks. Alcohol, tannin and aroma from the previous wine linger; rinse, rest and record before moving on.

Fifth, mistaking heat for body. The warmth of alcohol and the weight of the liquid are not the same thing. Sixth, recording only the first sip. The liquid changes in the mouth, and the second and third sips may show a different structure, especially in a just-opened bottle.

Practice

First, fix the variables. Using one glass, the same sip size and the same contact time, taste the same wine three times and record the differences. Second, taste the same wine at different temperatures and compare aroma, sweetness, acidity and alcohol, to learn how temperature changes structure.

Third, time the finish. Start a timer after swallowing or spitting, note when the flavour disappears, and compare with others. Fourth, taste blind. Score each structural dimension, then guess drink type and age, and finally check the answer; the aim is stable description.

Do not pursue volume. Tasting is about collecting information, not intake. When many wines are involved, use spitting and schedule rests.

Relationship to other steps

Sipping follows nosing. Expectations from the nose are confirmed or corrected in the mouth: a wine that smells sweet may taste dry, and one that seems closed may open on the palate. Record nose and palate separately so that one stage does not overwrite the other.

Sipping is also where acidity, body and finish are judged. Read them together: high acidity with a light body can be refreshing, but with a full body it can feel sharp; a long but coarse finish is not necessarily better than a short, clean one. Treating a single metric as a total score is the commonest simplification in tasting.

Common misconceptions

First, the more you drink, the more you understand. Larger sips let alcohol interfere with judgement; professional tasting emphasises small amounts and spitting.

Second, the first sip is the whole story. A just-opened wine may not have opened up, and the second sip, third sip or the decanted wine can all differ.

Third, sweetness equals sugar. Alcohol and glycerol also add roundness, and a dry wine can still feel sweet in texture.

Fourth, stronger tannin is better. The amount and quality of tannin must match body, acidity and flavour; coarse tannin breaks balance.

Fifth, a longer finish is always better. Judge the cleanliness and content of the finish; persistent bitterness or heat is not a virtue.

Start with the timing and temperature. From there, consider the equipment, then the scale it is practised at. That is how a practical judgment about Sipping is built.
